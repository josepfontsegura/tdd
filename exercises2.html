Exercises: 2. Decoding

Work out your answer before opening a solution. Each exercise has three options and exactly one correct answer; the solution explains the choice.

For the repetition code, the two alternatives are the messages $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, two ways of labelling two messages, with codewords $\boldsymbol{x}(1)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$; the decoder decides $\hat m$, and $P_{\rm e}(1)$, $P_{\rm e}(2)$ and $P_{\rm e}$ are the conditional and the average error probabilities. The rate $R$ counts information bits per channel use. On the bit-erasing channel an erased bit is written $?$. Channels are memoryless, and for additive channels the noise is independent of the input unless stated otherwise. We use $\ln$ for the natural logarithm and $\operatorname{erfc}(u)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_u^\infty e^{-t^2}\,dt$ for the complementary error function; answers may be left in terms of $\operatorname{erfc}$. Assign probability-zero decision boundaries arbitrarily; for ties with positive probability, follow the rule in the exercise. Symbols on the Gaussian channel represent amplitudes, with the SNR per symbol equal to the symbol energy divided by the noise variance; exponential-channel inputs represent energies, with the convention stated in each exercise. Statements that refer to an earlier exercise give its number on this page.

Exercise 2.1.

Three information bits are sent uncoded over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac15$, one bit per channel use, and the receiver takes each bit as received, so that a detected bit is wrong exactly when the channel flips it. The channel is memoryless. What is the probability that at least one of the three detected bits is wrong?

(a) $\tfrac35$
(b) $\tfrac{61}{125}$
(c) $\tfrac1{125}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

Each detected bit is correct with probability $1-p=\tfrac45$, and the three decisions are independent because the channel is memoryless. The probability that the three bits are correct is the product $\bigl(\tfrac45\bigr)^3=\tfrac{64}{125}$, so the probability that at least one is wrong is $1-\tfrac{64}{125}=\tfrac{61}{125}$. Expanding, $1-(1-p)^3=3p-3p^2+p^3$.

Option (a) adds the error probabilities of the three bits as if the three events were disjoint, and it counts the outputs with two or three wrong bits more than once. The terms $-3p^2+p^3$ of the expansion correct this. Option (c) is the probability $p^3$ that all three bits are wrong. With $N$ uncoded bits the probability of some error is $1-(1-p)^N$, which tends to one as $N$ grows, and this is the reason for coding.

Exercise 2.2.

A binary code has $16$ codewords of length $n=7$, so that each codeword is sent in seven channel uses and identifies one of $16$ equiprobable messages. What is the rate $R$ of the code, in information bits per channel use?

(a) $R=\tfrac47$
(b) $R=\tfrac{16}7$
(c) $R=\tfrac74$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

A code with $2^k$ codewords carries $k$ information bits per codeword, since each codeword identifies one of $2^k$ messages. Here $16=2^4$, so $k=4$, and the rate is $R=\tfrac kn=\tfrac47$ information bits per channel use.

Option (b) divides the number of codewords by the length, but the number of information bits is $\log_2 16=4$, not $16$. Option (c) is the inverse ratio $n/k$, which counts channel uses per information bit. For comparison, the repetition code of length $7$ has two codewords, so that $k=1$ and $R=\tfrac17$.

Exercise 2.3.

An encoder maps every block of $k=3$ information bits to $n_{\rm c}=6$ coded bits. The coded bits are mapped two at a time to the symbols of a 4-PAM constellation, so that each block is sent in $n=3$ channel uses. What are the binary rate $r$ of the encoder and the rate $R$ in information bits per channel use?

(a) $r=\tfrac12$ and $R=\tfrac12$
(b) $r=\tfrac12$ and $R=\tfrac14$
(c) $r=\tfrac12$ and $R=1$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The binary rate counts information bits per coded bit, $r=k/n_{\rm c}=\tfrac36=\tfrac12$. Each 4-PAM symbol carries $\log_2 4=2$ coded bits, so the six coded bits take $n=3$ channel uses, and the rate per channel use is $R=\tfrac kn=\tfrac33=1$, that is, $R=r\log_2M=\tfrac12\cdot2=1$.

Option (a) identifies the binary rate with the rate per channel use, which is correct only for binary constellations, where $n_{\rm c}=n$. Option (b) divides by $\log_2M$ instead of multiplying. A symbol of a larger constellation carries more coded bits, and therefore more information bits too. Uncoded 4-PAM would have $R=2$, and this encoder halves it.

Exercise 2.4.

Three information bits are sent uncoded over the bit-flipping channel, one bit per channel use. Seen as a code, this scheme maps $k=3$ information bits to codewords of $n=3$ bits. Which statement is correct?

(a) The rate is $R=1$, and every sequence of three bits is a codeword, so the receiver can never notice a flipped bit.
(b) The rate is $R=\tfrac13$, since every codeword has three bits.
(c) The rate is $R=1$, and a single flipped bit is noticed, because the received sequence is then not a codeword.

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The scheme has $2^3=8$ messages and $8$ codewords of length $3$, so $R=\tfrac kn=\tfrac33=1$. Since every sequence of three bits is a codeword, the receiver always sees a codeword, whatever the channel does. It cannot notice an error, and each flipped bit becomes a wrong information bit. Errors can be noticed only if some received sequences are not codewords, which requires a rate below one.

Option (b) is the rate of the repetition code of length $3$, which has only the two codewords $000$ and $111$ and carries one information bit. Option (c) describes a code with redundancy. With the repetition code, for instance, the received sequence $010$ is not a codeword, so a single flip is noticed.

Exercise 2.5.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac15$. The decoder takes the majority vote of the three received bits. What is its error probability $P_{\rm e}$?

(a) $\tfrac{12}{125}$
(b) $\tfrac{13}{125}$
(c) $\tfrac1{125}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

When $m=1$ is sent, the majority vote errs when at least two of the three bits are flipped. Exactly two flips can occur in three positions, each pattern with probability $p^2(1-p)=\tfrac1{25}\cdot\tfrac45=\tfrac4{125}$, and three flips have probability $p^3=\tfrac1{125}$. Hence $P_{\rm e}(1)=3\cdot\tfrac4{125}+\tfrac1{125}=\tfrac{13}{125}$, which is $p^2(3-2p)$. By symmetry $P_{\rm e}(2)=\tfrac{13}{125}$ too, and the average is $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac{13}{125}$, about half of the value $p=\tfrac{25}{125}$ of a single bit.

Option (a) forgets the output with three flips, which the majority vote also decodes wrongly. Option (c) counts only that output, as if two flips could still be corrected. With three bits, the majority vote corrects one flip and no more.

Exercise 2.6.

On the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p$, where $0< p< 1$, one of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent either once, as a single bit that the receiver takes as received, or with the repetition code of length $3$ decoded by the majority vote. For which values of $p$ is the error probability of the repetition code smaller than that of the single bit?

(a) Only for $0< p< \tfrac13$
(b) For every $p$ with $0< p< 1$
(c) Exactly for $0< p< \tfrac12$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The single bit has error probability $p$, and the repetition code with the majority vote has $p^2(3-2p)$. Their difference factors as $p-p^2(3-2p)=p\,(1-3p+2p^2)=p\,(1-p)(1-2p)$, which is positive exactly when $p< \tfrac12$, since $0< p< 1$. At $p=\tfrac12$ both error probabilities are $\tfrac12$, and for $p>\tfrac12$ the majority vote makes things worse.

Option (a) comes from the approximation $P_{\rm e}\approx3p^2$, which keeps only the leading term and is accurate only for small $p$. Solving $3p^2< p$ gives $p< \tfrac13$, but at $p=\tfrac25$, for instance, the exact value is $\tfrac{44}{125}< \tfrac25$. Option (b) ignores that, when $p>\tfrac12$, a received bit is more often wrong than right, so a majority of received bits is more likely to be a majority of wrong bits.

Exercise 2.7.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $n=5$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=00000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=11111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac15$. The decoder takes the majority vote of the five received bits. What is its error probability $P_{\rm e}$?

(a) $\tfrac{32}{625}$
(b) $\tfrac{821}{3125}$
(c) $\tfrac{181}{3125}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

By symmetry $P_{\rm e}(1)=P_{\rm e}(2)$, and the majority vote of five bits errs when at least three of them are flipped. Counting the positions of the flips, $$P_{\rm e}=\binom53p^3(1-p)^2+\binom54p^4(1-p)+p^5=\frac{10\cdot16+5\cdot4+1}{5^5}=\frac{181}{3125}.$$ This is about $0.058$, against $\tfrac{13}{125}\approx0.104$ for the length $3$ and $\tfrac15$ for a single bit.

Option (a) keeps only the first term, $10p^3(1-p)^2=\tfrac{160}{3125}=\tfrac{32}{625}$, and forgets the outputs with four or five flips. Option (b) assumes that two flips already cause an error, $1-(1-p)^5-5p(1-p)^4=\tfrac{821}{3125}$, but with five bits the majority vote corrects up to two flips.

Exercise 2.8.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $n=4$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=0000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=1111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac1{10}$. The decoder takes the majority vote of the four received bits and tosses a fair coin when two of them are $0$ and two are $1$. What is its error probability $P_{\rm e}$?

(a) $\tfrac7{250}$
(b) $\tfrac{523}{10000}$
(c) $\tfrac{37}{10000}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

When $m=1$ is sent, the decoder errs for sure when three or four bits are flipped, and with probability $\tfrac12$ when exactly two are, because the vote is then tied. Hence $$P_{\rm e}(1)=\tfrac12\binom42p^2(1-p)^2+\binom43p^3(1-p)+p^4=\frac{243+36+1}{10^4}=\frac7{250},$$ and $P_{\rm e}(2)$ and the average take the same value. This is exactly the error probability $p^2(3-2p)=\tfrac{28}{1000}$ of the length $3$. The fourth copy does not help the majority vote, which is the ML decoder here, and it lowers the rate from $\tfrac13$ to $\tfrac14$.

Option (b) counts every tie as an error, $\tfrac{486+36+1}{10^4}$, and option (c) counts every tie as a correct decision, $\tfrac{36+1}{10^4}$. The coin is right half of the time.

Exercise 2.9.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $n$, whose codewords are $0\cdots0$ and $1\cdots1$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac12$. What is the error probability $P_{\rm e}$ of an ML decoder, whatever rule it uses to break ties between equal likelihoods?

(a) $P_{\rm e}=2^{-n}$, which tends to zero as $n$ grows
(b) $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12$ for every $n$
(c) $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\,2^{-n}$, as on the bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $\tfrac12$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

With $p=\tfrac12$, every received sequence $\boldsymbol{y}$ of $n$ bits has the likelihood $W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})=2^{-n}$ under both codewords, so the output is independent of the message and carries no information about it. Every decoder is then an ML decoder, and with equiprobable messages every decoder has $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12$, whatever the length. The channel is useless, and repetition cannot help.

Option (a) would require each copy to carry some information, as it does when $p< \tfrac12$. Option (c) is the error probability of the bit-erasing channel, where an unerased bit is always correct. An erased bit is known to be unreliable, whereas a received bit of this channel looks reliable and is right only half of the time.

Exercise 2.10.

One of two messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac15$. The receiver observes $\boldsymbol{y}=010$. What are the likelihoods $W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)$ and $W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)$ of the two codewords?

(a) $W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)=\tfrac{16}{125}$ and $W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)=\tfrac4{125}$
(b) $W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)=\tfrac15$ and $W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)=\tfrac1{25}$
(c) $W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)=\tfrac4{125}$ and $W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)=\tfrac{16}{125}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The channel is memoryless, so the likelihood of a sequence is the product of the laws of its symbols. For $000$, the first and third bits arrive unchanged and the second is flipped, so $W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)=(1-p)\cdot p\cdot(1-p)=\tfrac45\cdot\tfrac15\cdot\tfrac45=\tfrac{16}{125}$. For $111$, the first and third bits are flipped and the second arrives unchanged, so $W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)=p\cdot(1-p)\cdot p=\tfrac4{125}$. The ratio $W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)/W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)=\tfrac p{1-p}=\tfrac14$ is below one, so the ML decoder decides $\hat m=1$, the majority of the received bits.

Option (b) multiplies only the factors of the flipped positions and forgets the factor $1-p$ of each bit that arrives unchanged. The likelihood is the probability of the whole received sequence, so every position contributes a factor. Option (c) exchanges the two codewords.

Exercise 2.11.

One of two messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $n=5$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=00000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=11111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac1{10}$. The receiver observes $\boldsymbol{y}=11010$. What is the likelihood ratio $W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}(2))/W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}(1))$?

(a) $729$
(b) $\tfrac19$
(c) $9$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

Let $d=3$ be the number of ones in $\boldsymbol{y}$. The codeword $11111$ differs from $\boldsymbol{y}$ in the $n-d=2$ positions of the zeros, and $00000$ differs from it in the $d=3$ positions of the ones, so $W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}(2))=p^2(1-p)^3$ and $W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}(1))=p^3(1-p)^2$. Their ratio is $$\frac{W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}(2))}{W(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}(1))}=\frac{p^2(1-p)^3}{p^3(1-p)^2}=\frac{1-p}{p}=9,$$ and in general $\bigl((1-p)/p\bigr)^{2d-n}$. It exceeds one, so the ML decoder decides $\hat m=2$, which is the majority of the received bits.

Option (a) counts a factor $9$ in favor of $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$ for each of the three ones, $9^3=729$, and forgets that each of the two zeros counts a factor $9$ in favor of $\boldsymbol{x}(1)$. Only the difference $d-(n-d)=1$ between the two counts matters. Option (b) is the inverse ratio.

Exercise 2.12.

A code with $k=1$ and $n=5$ has the two codewords $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=00000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=11100$, and it is used with equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p$, where $0< p< \tfrac12$. The receiver observes $\boldsymbol{y}=10100$. What does the ML decoder decide?

(a) $\hat m=1$, because three of the five received bits are $0$
(b) $\hat m=2$, because $\boldsymbol{y}$ differs from $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$ in one position and from $\boldsymbol{x}(1)$ in two
(c) It depends on the value of $p$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

On the bit-flipping channel, a codeword that differs from $\boldsymbol{y}$ in $d$ positions has likelihood $p^d(1-p)^{n-d}=(1-p)^n\bigl(p/(1-p)\bigr)^d$, which decreases with $d$ when $p< \tfrac12$. The ML decoder therefore chooses the codeword at the smallest Hamming distance from $\boldsymbol{y}$. Here $\boldsymbol{y}=10100$ differs from $00000$ in positions $1$ and $3$, and from $11100$ only in position $2$, so the decoder decides $\hat m=2$. The two likelihoods are $p^2(1-p)^3$ and $p(1-p)^4$, with ratio $(1-p)/p>1$.

Option (a) applies the majority vote, which is the ML decoder of the repetition code only, because its codewords are constant. Here the last two positions are $0$ in both codewords, so they say nothing about the message. Option (c) is wrong because the comparison of the two distances gives the same decision for every $p$ with $0< p< \tfrac12$.

Exercise 2.13.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac45$. What is the error probability $P_{\rm e}$ of the ML decoder?

(a) $\tfrac{13}{125}$
(b) $\tfrac{112}{125}$
(c) $\tfrac12$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

With $d$ ones in $\boldsymbol{y}$, the likelihood ratio of $111$ against $000$ is $\bigl((1-p)/p\bigr)^{2d-3}=\bigl(\tfrac14\bigr)^{2d-3}$, and its base is now smaller than one. The ratio exceeds one when $2d-3< 0$, that is, when $d\leq1$, so the ML decoder decides $\hat m=2$ when most received bits are $0$. It is the minority vote, that is, the majority vote after flipping every received bit. After that flip each bit is correct with probability $p=\tfrac45$, so the decoder faces a bit-flipping channel with flip probability $1-p=\tfrac15$, and $P_{\rm e}=(1-p)^2\bigl(3-2(1-p)\bigr)=\tfrac1{25}\cdot\tfrac{13}5=\tfrac{13}{125}$.

Option (b) is the error probability $p^2(3-2p)=\tfrac{16}{25}\cdot\tfrac75=\tfrac{112}{125}$ of the majority vote, which is not the ML decoder when $p>\tfrac12$. Option (c) treats the channel as useless, but only $p=\tfrac12$ is useless. A channel that flips with probability $\tfrac45$ is as informative as one that flips with probability $\tfrac15$, once the decoder takes it into account.

Exercise 2.14.

One of two messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac15$. The priors are $\Pr(m=1)=\tfrac1{10}$ and $\Pr(m=2)=\tfrac9{10}$. For which received sequences does the MAP decoder decide $\hat m=2$?

(a) When at least two received bits are $1$, as the majority vote
(b) When at least one received bit is $1$
(c) For every received sequence, including $000$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

Let $d$ be the number of ones in $\boldsymbol{y}$. The MAP decoder decides $\hat m=2$ when $\Pr(m=2)\,W(\boldsymbol{y}|111)>\Pr(m=1)\,W(\boldsymbol{y}|000)$, that is, when $9\,p^{3-d}(1-p)^d>p^d(1-p)^{3-d}$. Collecting the powers, the condition is $$9>\Bigl(\frac{1-p}{p}\Bigr)^{3-2d}=4^{3-2d}.$$ For $d=0$ the right-hand side is $64$, and the decoder decides $\hat m=1$. For $d=1$ it is $4$, below $9$, and for $d=2$ and $d=3$ it is smaller still, so the decoder decides $\hat m=2$ as soon as one received bit is $1$.

Option (a) is the ML decoder, which ignores the priors. At $d=1$ the two products are $\tfrac9{10}\cdot\tfrac4{125}=\tfrac{36}{1250}$ for $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$ and $\tfrac1{10}\cdot\tfrac{16}{125}=\tfrac{16}{1250}$ for $\boldsymbol{x}(1)$, so two flips of the frequent codeword are more likely than one flip of the rare one. Option (c) would need the prior odds $9$ to exceed $4^3=64$, which happens only for $\Pr(m=2)>\tfrac{64}{65}$.

Exercise 2.15.

In the setting of Exercise 2.14, what is the error probability $P_{\rm e}$ of the MAP decoder?

(a) $\tfrac{13}{125}$
(b) $\tfrac1{10}$
(c) $\tfrac7{125}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

By Exercise 2.14, the MAP decoder decides $\hat m=2$ unless $\boldsymbol{y}=000$. When $m=1$ is sent, it errs as soon as one bit is flipped, so $P_{\rm e}(1)=1-(1-p)^3=1-\tfrac{64}{125}=\tfrac{61}{125}$. When $m=2$ is sent, it errs only if the three bits are flipped, so $P_{\rm e}(2)=p^3=\tfrac1{125}$. Weighting with the priors, $$P_{\rm e}=\tfrac1{10}\cdot\tfrac{61}{125}+\tfrac9{10}\cdot\tfrac1{125}=\tfrac{70}{1250}=\tfrac7{125}.$$

Option (a) is the error probability $p^2(3-2p)$ of the majority vote, which is the same for every prior because its two conditional errors are equal. The MAP decoder almost halves it, at the price of a large $P_{\rm e}(1)$ for the rare message. Option (b) is the error probability of the decoder that ignores the output and always decides $\hat m=2$, which errs exactly when $m=1$ is sent. No decoder beats MAP, and both alternatives are worse.

Exercise 2.16.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=000$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=111$, over the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac15$. A decoder decides $\hat m=2$ only when $\boldsymbol{y}=111$, and $\hat m=1$ otherwise. What is its error probability $P_{\rm e}$?

(a) $\tfrac{31}{125}$
(b) $\tfrac1{125}$
(c) $\tfrac{13}{125}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

When $m=1$ is sent, the decoder errs only if the three bits are flipped, so $P_{\rm e}(1)=p^3=\tfrac1{125}$. When $m=2$ is sent, it errs unless the three bits arrive unchanged, so $P_{\rm e}(2)=1-(1-p)^3=\tfrac{61}{125}$. With equiprobable messages, $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac1{125}+\tfrac{61}{125}\bigr)=\tfrac{31}{125}$.

Option (b) is $P_{\rm e}(1)$ alone. The two conditional errors of this decoder are very different, and both enter the average. Option (c) is the error probability of the majority vote, which is the ML decoder and, with equiprobable messages, also the MAP decoder. No decoder has a smaller average error probability, and this one is more than twice as bad. The same unanimity rule is the ML decoder on the exponential channel, where the noise never lowers an output, but not on the bit-flipping channel, where both kinds of flip occur.

Exercise 2.17.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $000$ and $111$, and decoded with the ML decoder, which tosses a fair coin when the two likelihoods are equal. Two channels are compared: the bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $\tfrac1{10}$, which never flips a bit and writes an erased bit as $?$, and the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $\tfrac1{10}$. What are the two error probabilities, in this order?

(a) $\tfrac1{1000}$ and $\tfrac7{250}$
(b) $\tfrac7{250}$ and $\tfrac7{250}$
(c) $\tfrac1{2000}$ and $\tfrac7{250}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

On the bit-erasing channel an unerased bit is always correct, so one unerased bit identifies the codeword. The decoder errs only when the three bits are erased, with probability $p^3=\tfrac1{1000}$, and the coin is wrong, with probability $\tfrac12$, so $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12p^3=\tfrac1{2000}$. On the bit-flipping channel the ML decoder is the majority vote, which errs with two or three flips, $P_{\rm e}=p^2(3-2p)=\tfrac1{100}\cdot\tfrac{28}{10}=\tfrac7{250}$, which is $56$ times larger.

Option (a) identifies the probability $p^3$ of a total erasure with the error probability, forgetting that the coin is right half of the time. Option (b) treats an erasure as a flip. An erasure tells the decoder which position is unreliable, while a flipped bit looks like a correct one, so the same number of corrupted bits does much less harm on the erasure channel.

Exercise 2.18.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $n$ over the bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=\tfrac12$, which never flips a bit. The ML decoder tosses a fair coin when every bit is erased. What is the smallest $n$ for which $P_{\rm e}\leq\tfrac1{100}$, and what is the rate of that code?

(a) $n=7$ and $R=\tfrac17$
(b) $n=6$ and $R=\tfrac16$
(c) No length reaches it, because with $p=\tfrac12$ the channel is useless

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The ML decoder errs only when the $n$ bits are erased and the coin is wrong, so $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12p^n=2^{-(n+1)}$. For $n=5$ this is $\tfrac1{64}>\tfrac1{100}$, and for $n=6$ it is $\tfrac1{128}\leq\tfrac1{100}$. The smallest length is $n=6$, with $R=\tfrac16$.

Option (a) requires the probability $p^n$ of a total erasure to be below $\tfrac1{100}$ and forgets the factor $\tfrac12$ of the coin, which saves one repetition here. Option (c) confuses this channel with the bit-flipping channel with $p=\tfrac12$, which is useless. Here half of the bits arrive, and each of them is correct.

Exercise 2.19.

(b) $\tfrac9{32}$
(c) $\tfrac9{16}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

An unerased bit is always correct, whatever the value of $p$, so the ML decoder follows any unerased bit and is then right for sure. It can only err when both bits are erased, which has probability $p^2=\tfrac9{16}$, and the coin is then wrong half of the time, so $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12p^2=\tfrac9{32}$.

The hard receiver detects the sign of each output, so that each detected bit is wrong with probability $p$, independently of the other. It follows the detected bits when they agree and tosses a fair coin when they disagree. What is its error probability?

(a) $p$
(b) $p^2$
(c) $2p-p^2$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

An error occurs when both detected bits are wrong, with probability $p^2$, or when exactly one of them is wrong, with probability $2p(1-p)$, and the coin then chooses the wrong message, with probability $\tfrac12$. The total is $p^2+p(1-p)=p$, the error probability of sending the bit once. With hard decisions the second copy is wasted, whereas the soft receiver has $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}}\bigr)$, the gain of $3$ dB of two repetitions.

Option (b) counts only the case of two wrong bits and assumes that a disagreement is always resolved correctly. Option (c) counts every disagreement as an error, $p^2+2p(1-p)$, and forgets that the coin is right half of the time.

Exercise 2.38.

One of two messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$ on the real Gaussian channel, with codewords $(-A,-A,-A)$ and $(A,A,A)$, where $A>0$. The hard receiver takes the majority of the signs of the three outputs, and the soft receiver decides by the sign of their sum. Which condition on the outputs guarantees that the two decisions are the same?

(a) That the sum of the outputs is positive
(b) That the three outputs have the same sign
(c) No condition is needed, since both receivers use the same three outputs

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

If the three outputs have the same sign, their sum has that sign too, and both receivers decide the same message. If two outputs are positive and one is negative, the hard receiver decides $\hat m=2$, while the soft receiver decides $\hat m=1$ when the negative output is large enough, as for $\bigl(\tfrac A2,\tfrac A2,-2A\bigr)$, whose sum is $-A$. The same happens with the signs exchanged. The condition is sufficient but not necessary, since the decisions also agree for outputs such as $\bigl(A,A,-\tfrac A2\bigr)$.

Option (a) fails for $\bigl(A,-\tfrac A4,-\tfrac A4\bigr)$, whose sum is $\tfrac A2>0$, while the hard receiver sees two negative signs and decides $\hat m=1$. Option (c) ignores that the hard receiver keeps only the sign of each output and discards its magnitude.

Exercise 2.39.

The lecture notes show that, at high SNR, the hard receiver of the repetition code of odd length $n$ on the real Gaussian channel needs about $2n/(n+1)$ times the SNR of the soft receiver to reach the same error probability. How large is this loss for $n=3$, and what is its limit as $n$ grows?

(a) About $4.8$ dB for $n=3$, growing without bound
(b) No loss, $0$ dB for every $n$, since both receivers use the same code
(c) About $1.8$ dB for $n=3$, tending to $3$ dB

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

For $n=3$ the factor is $\tfrac{2\cdot3}{3+1}=\tfrac32$, that is, $10\log_{10}\tfrac32\approx1.8$ dB. As $n$ grows, $2n/(n+1)$ tends to $2$, that is, $3$ dB. At high SNR the hard receiver fails when $(n+1)/2$ detected bits are wrong, and the probability of each wrong bit decays like $e^{-\mathrm{SNR}/2}$, so its error probability decays like $e^{-(n+1)\mathrm{SNR}/4}$. The soft receiver decays like $e^{-n\,\mathrm{SNR}/2}$, and the ratio of the two exponents is $2n/(n+1)$.

Option (a) is the gain $10\log_{10}3\approx4.8$ dB of the soft receiver with $n=3$ over a single symbol, which is a different comparison. Option (b) ignores that the detector keeps only the sign of each output and discards its magnitude, which the soft receiver uses.

Exercise 2.40.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=(0,0,0)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=(2,2,2)$, over the channel $y_i=x_i+z_i$ with independent exponential noise samples $z_i\geq0$ of mean $\mu$, where $e^{-2/\mu}=\tfrac12$. The ML decoder uses the likelihoods of the whole output. The majority decoder first decides each symbol from its own output, $\hat x_i=2$ when $y_i\geq2$ and $\hat x_i=0$ otherwise, and then takes the majority of the three symbol decisions. For which of these outputs do the two decoders decide differently?

(a) $\boldsymbol{y}=(3,\,5,\,4)$
(b) $\boldsymbol{y}=(4,\,1,\,3)$
(c) $\boldsymbol{y}=(1,\,1,\,7)$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The likelihood of $\boldsymbol{x}(1)$ is positive for every output, since the outputs are nonnegative. The likelihood of $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$ is zero as soon as one output is below $2$, because the noise never lowers a symbol, and otherwise the likelihood ratio is $e^{6/\mu}=8>1$. The ML decoder therefore decides $\hat m=2$ exactly when every output is at least $2$, while the majority decoder decides $\hat m=2$ when at least two outputs are at least $2$. The two rules differ only when exactly two outputs reach $2$.

For $\boldsymbol{y}=(4,1,3)$, the output $y_2=1$ has probability zero under $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$, so the ML decoder decides $\hat m=1$, while the majority decoder sees $(2,0,2)$ and decides $\hat m=2$. For option (a) both decide $\hat m=2$, since every output reaches $2$, and for option (c) both decide $\hat m=1$, since only one output does.

Exercise 2.41.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=(0,0,0)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=(2,2,2)$, over the channel $y_i=x_i+z_i$ with independent exponential noise samples $z_i\geq0$ of mean $\mu$, where $e^{-2/\mu}=\tfrac14$. The majority decoder decides $\hat x_i=2$ when $y_i\geq2$ and $\hat x_i=0$ otherwise, and then takes the majority of the three symbol decisions. What is its error probability $P_{\rm e}$?

(a) $\tfrac5{64}$
(b) $\tfrac5{32}$
(c) $\tfrac1{128}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

Each noise sample reaches $2$ with probability $q=e^{-2/\mu}=\tfrac14$, independently of the others. When $m=1$ is sent, the majority decoder errs when at least two of the three samples reach $2$, so $P_{\rm e}(1)=3q^2(1-q)+q^3=\tfrac9{64}+\tfrac1{64}=\tfrac{10}{64}$. When $m=2$ is sent, every output is at least $2$, every symbol decision is $2$ and the decoder never errs, $P_{\rm e}(2)=0$. The average is $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{10}{64}=\tfrac5{64}$.

Option (b) is $P_{\rm e}(1)$ alone, without the average with $P_{\rm e}(2)=0$. Option (c) is the error probability $\tfrac12q^3$ of the ML decoder, which decides $\hat m=2$ only when the three outputs reach $2$. The majority decoder is ten times worse here, because it errs as soon as two samples reach $2$.

Exercise 2.42.

One of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $3$, whose codewords are $\boldsymbol{x}(1)=(0,0,0)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(2)=(2,2,2)$, over the channel $y_i=x_i+z_i$ with independent exponential noise samples $z_i\geq0$ of mean $\mu$, where $e^{-2/\mu}=\tfrac12$. A receiver first detects each symbol, $\hat x_i=2$ when $y_i\geq2$ and $\hat x_i=0$ otherwise, and then applies the ML decoder to the three detected symbols. What is its conditional error probability $P_{\rm e}(1)$ when $m=1$ is sent?

(a) $\tfrac12$
(b) $\tfrac78$
(c) $\tfrac18$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

From the sent symbols to the detected symbols, a $2$ is always detected as $2$, because $y_i=2+z_i\geq2$, and a $0$ is detected as $2$ with probability $\Pr(z_i\geq2)=\tfrac12$. When some detected symbol is $0$, the likelihood of $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$ is zero, since a $2$ is never detected as $0$, and the decoder decides $\hat m=1$. When the three detected symbols are $2$, the likelihoods are $1$ for $\boldsymbol{x}(2)$ and $\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)^3$ for $\boldsymbol{x}(1)$, and it decides $\hat m=2$. This receiver decides $\hat m=2$ exactly when every output reaches $2$, as the ML decoder of the outputs does, and $P_{\rm e}(1)=\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)^3=\tfrac18$.

Option (a) is the conditional error of the majority decoder, which votes on the detected symbols as if the channel were symmetric. The loss of the majority decoder comes from the vote, not from the symbol decisions, because on this channel the detected symbols keep everything the ML decoder needs. Option (b) is the probability that at least one sample reaches $2$.

Exercise 2.43.

A code maps $k=4$ information bits to codewords of $n=7$ symbols, each of them $-A$ or $+A$, so that every symbol has energy $E_{\rm s}=A^2$. What is the energy per information bit $E_{\rm b}$?

(a) $E_{\rm b}=\tfrac47A^2$
(b) $E_{\rm b}=\tfrac74A^2$
(c) $E_{\rm b}=7A^2$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

Each codeword has $n=7$ symbols of energy $A^2$, that is, energy $7A^2$, and it carries $k=4$ information bits, so $E_{\rm b}=\tfrac{nE_{\rm s}}k=\tfrac{E_{\rm s}}R=\tfrac74A^2$, with $R=\tfrac47$. A rate below one makes each information bit more expensive than a symbol.

Option (a) multiplies by the rate instead of dividing by it. Option (c) is the energy of a whole codeword, which is the energy per information bit only when $k=1$, as for the repetition code of length $7$.

Exercise 2.44.

On the real Gaussian channel with a fixed noise variance $\sigma^2$, one information bit is sent with the repetition code and the ML decoder, at a fixed energy per information bit $E_{\rm b}$. The length is increased from $n=1$ to $n=4$, and the symbols are scaled accordingly. What happens to the SNR per symbol and to the error probability?

(a) The SNR per symbol is divided by $4$, and the error probability does not change
(b) The SNR per symbol does not change, and the error probability decreases, since $n$ repetitions are worth $n$ times the SNR
(c) The SNR per symbol is divided by $4$, and the error probability increases

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

For the repetition code $E_{\rm b}=nE_{\rm s}$, so at fixed $E_{\rm b}$ each of the four symbols has energy $E_{\rm s}=E_{\rm b}/4$, and the SNR per symbol is divided by $4$. The error probability is $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{n\,\mathrm{SNR}/2}\bigr)$, and $n\,\mathrm{SNR}=nE_{\rm s}/\sigma^2=E_{\rm b}/\sigma^2$ does not depend on $n$, so $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{E_{\rm b}/(2\sigma^2)}\bigr)$ for every length.

Option (b) keeps the energy per symbol fixed, which is the comparison at fixed $E_{\rm s}$, where the gain is paid with $n$ times more energy per information bit. Option (c) forgets that the decoder collects the energy of the four symbols. At equal energy per information bit, repetition gains nothing, and it divides the rate by $4$.

Exercise 2.45.

On the real Gaussian channel with noise variance $\sigma^2=2$, one information bit is sent in one of two ways: once, with the symbols $\pm2$, or with the repetition code of length $4$, with the symbols $\pm1$ and the ML decoder. Both schemes have $E_{\rm b}=4$. What are their error probabilities, in this order?

(a) $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1)$ and $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(2)$
(b) $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1)$ and $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)$
(c) $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1)$ and $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1)$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

Sent once with $\pm2$, the bit has $\mathrm{SNR}=\tfrac42=2$ and $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}/2}\bigr)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1)$. With four repetitions of $\pm1$, the SNR per symbol is $\tfrac12$, and $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{4\cdot\tfrac12/2}\bigr)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1)$ too. In both cases $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{E_{\rm b}/(2\sigma^2)}\bigr)$ with $E_{\rm b}=4$.

Option (a) multiplies by $n=4$ the SNR of the uncoded symbol $\pm2$, but the repetition code does not send four symbols of that energy. Option (b) forgets the factor $n$ and uses the SNR of a single symbol $\pm1$.

Exercise 2.46.

On the real Gaussian channel, one of two equiprobable messages, $m\in\{1,2\}$ or equivalently $D\in\{0,1\}$, is sent with the repetition code of length $n$ and the ML decoder, with fixed symbols $\pm A$ and a fixed noise variance $\sigma^2$. Which statement describes what happens as $n$ grows?

(a) The error probability tends to zero, while the energy per information bit stays equal to $A^2$
(b) The error probability tends to zero, the rate $\tfrac1n$ tends to zero, and the energy per information bit $nA^2$ grows without bound
(c) The error probability first decreases and then grows, because the noise of the $n$ samples accumulates in the sum

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

At fixed $A$ and $\sigma^2$, $P_{\rm e}=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{n\,\mathrm{SNR}/2}\bigr)$ decreases to zero as $n$ grows, because $\operatorname{erfc}$ decreases to zero. The price is the rate $R=\tfrac1n$, which also tends to zero, and the energy, since each information bit costs $E_{\rm b}=nA^2$, the energy of $n$ symbols.

Option (a) confuses the energy per symbol with the energy per information bit. Option (c) is right that the noise of the sum grows, with variance $n\sigma^2$, but the signal part $nA$ grows faster. The ratio of the squared mean to the variance of the sum is $n^2A^2/(n\sigma^2)=n\,\mathrm{SNR}$, which grows with $n$.

Exercise 2.47.

For the bit-flipping channel with $p=\tfrac1{10}$, the lecture notes report that the repetition code needs $n=23$, that is, $R=\tfrac1{23}$, to reach an error probability below $10^{-6}$, and that the capacity of this channel is $C\approx0.53$ bits per channel use. Which statement is correct?

(a) For every rate below $C$ there are codes whose error probability is as small as we wish, so some code of rate $\tfrac12$ has an error probability below $10^{-6}$
(b) Any error probability below $10^{-6}$ requires a rate close to zero, as for repetition
(c) Every code of rate below $C$ has an error probability below $10^{-6}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

Shannon's answer, which Topic 3 develops, is that every channel has a capacity $C$ and that, for every rate below $C$, there are codes whose error probability is as small as we wish. Since $\tfrac12< 0.53$, some code of rate $\tfrac12$, more than ten times the rate of repetition, has an error probability below $10^{-6}$. Such codes are long, and they are not repetition codes.

Option (b) extends to every code the behavior of repetition, whose reliability is paid with a vanishing rate. Option (c) turns a statement about the existence of good codes into a statement about every code. The repetition code of length $3$, for instance, has rate $\tfrac13< C$ and error probability $\tfrac7{250}$.

Exercise 2.48.

The parity code of the lecture notes encodes two information bits $(D_1,D_2)$ into the three bits $D_1$, $D_2$ and their sum modulo $2$. What are its rate, and the smallest number of positions in which two of its codewords differ?

(a) $R=\tfrac32$, and any two codewords differ in exactly $2$ positions
(b) $R=\tfrac23$, and some pairs of codewords differ in a single position
(c) $R=\tfrac23$, and any two codewords differ in exactly $2$ positions

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The code carries $k=2$ information bits in $n=3$ bits, so $R=\tfrac23$. Its four codewords are $000$, $011$, $101$ and $110$, the sequences of three bits with an even number of ones. Changing a single bit changes the parity, so no two codewords differ in exactly one position, and comparing them shows that any two differ in exactly two positions. A single flipped bit therefore produces a sequence that is not a codeword, and it is noticed, although it cannot be located.

Option (a) inverts the rate, which counts information bits per channel use. Codewords that differ in a single position, as in option (b), occur in the code of all eight sequences of three bits, which has rate $1$ and no redundancy, not in the parity code.

Exercise 2.49.

The parity code maps two information bits $(D_1,D_2)$ to the three bits $D_1$, $D_2$ and their sum modulo $2$, and each bit is sent as $-A$ for $0$ and $+A$ for $1$. What are the squared minimum Euclidean distance $d_{\min}^2$ between its codewords and its energy per information bit $E_{\rm b}$?

(a) $d_{\min}^2=8A^2$ and $E_{\rm b}=\tfrac32A^2$
(b) $d_{\min}^2=4A^2$ and $E_{\rm b}=\tfrac32A^2$
(c) $d_{\min}^2=8A^2$ and $E_{\rm b}=3A^2$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

Two codewords differ in exactly two positions, and in each of them the symbols are $-A$ and $+A$, at squared distance $(2A)^2=4A^2$. Hence $d_{\min}^2=2\cdot4A^2=8A^2$. A codeword has energy $3A^2$ and carries two information bits, so $E_{\rm b}=\tfrac32A^2$. The ratio $d_{\min}^2/E_{\rm b}=\tfrac{16}3$ is larger than the value $4$ of uncoded 2-PAM and of the repetition code, by a factor $\tfrac43$ at equal energy per information bit.

Option (b) takes the squared distance $4A^2$ between two symbols, as if two codewords could differ in a single position. Option (c) divides the energy of a codeword by one information bit, as for the repetition code, instead of by two.

Exercise 2.50.

The receiver observes $\boldsymbol{y}=\bigl(A,\,\tfrac A2,\,A\bigr)$. Which codeword does the ML decoder choose?

(a) $(+A,+A,+A)$
(b) $(+A,-A,+A)$
(c) $(+A,+A,-A)$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

On the Gaussian channel the ML decoder chooses the codeword nearest to $\boldsymbol{y}$ in Euclidean distance. The squared distances from $\boldsymbol{y}=\bigl(1,\tfrac12,1\bigr)A$ are $\bigl(4+\tfrac94+4\bigr)A^2=\tfrac{41}4A^2$ to $(-A,-A,-A)$, $\bigl(4+\tfrac14+0\bigr)A^2=\tfrac{17}4A^2$ to $(-A,+A,+A)$, $\bigl(0+\tfrac94+0\bigr)A^2=\tfrac94A^2$ to $(+A,-A,+A)$ and $\bigl(0+\tfrac14+4\bigr)A^2=\tfrac{17}4A^2$ to $(+A,+A,-A)$.
