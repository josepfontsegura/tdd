Exercises: 3. Mutual information and capacity

Work out your answer before opening a solution. Each exercise has three options and exactly one correct answer; the solution explains the choice.

Entropies are in bits and mutual informations and capacities in bits per channel use, with $\log_2$ throughout and $\ln$ for the natural logarithm. The binary entropy function is $h_2(p)=-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)$, and Table 3.1 of the lecture notes gives its values and a few logarithms for hand calculations. Channels are memoryless. Terms with zero probability count as zero in every entropy. On the bit-erasing channel an erased bit is written $?$. For additive channels the noise is independent of the input, $E_{\rm s}$ is the symbol energy, $\sigma^2$ the noise variance (the total variance on the complex channel, half of it per real component) and $\mathrm{SNR}=E_{\rm s}/\sigma^2$. Reliable transmission at a rate means that the error probability can be made as small as desired by codes of increasing length at that rate. Values quoted from Tables 3.4 and 3.5 of the notes are rounded. A noise uniform on an interval has a density equal to one over the length of the interval there and zero elsewhere, and a channel with a discrete input $X$ and a continuous output has the output density $f_Y(y)=\sum_x\Pr(x)W(y|x)$, with $I(X;Y)=h(Y)-h(Z)$ for additive noise. Statements that refer to an earlier exercise give its number on this page.

Exercise 3.1.

A source produces one of five symbols with probabilities $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac18$, $\tfrac1{16}$ and $\tfrac1{16}$. What is its entropy $H(X)$, in bits?

(a) $\tfrac{15}{8}$
(b) $2$
(c) $\log_25$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

By the definition of the entropy, each symbol contributes $-\Pr(x)\log_2\Pr(x)$, and the logarithms of these probabilities are the integers $-1$, $-2$, $-3$, $-4$ and $-4$. Hence $H(X)=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac1{16}\cdot4+\tfrac1{16}\cdot4=\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac38+\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac{15}8$ bits.

Option (c), $\log_25\approx2.32$, is the largest entropy of a five-symbol source, reached only when the five symbols are equiprobable. Option (b) would be the entropy of four equiprobable symbols. An unequal distribution always has less entropy than the equiprobable one on the same alphabet, and here the gap is about $0.45$ bits.

Exercise 3.2.

Three sources use the same alphabet of four symbols, with probabilities $\{\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\}$, $\{\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\}$ and $\{\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0,0\}$. Which of them has the largest entropy?

(a) The source with probabilities $\{\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\}$, because its probabilities are the most spread out.
(b) The equiprobable source, with $H(X)=2$ bits.
(c) The source with probabilities $\{\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0,0\}$, because two of its symbols are never used.

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The entropy of an alphabet of $M$ symbols is at most $\log_2M$, with equality exactly for the equiprobable distribution. Here $\log_24=2$ bits for the first source. The second source has entropy $\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot3=\tfrac74$ bits, and the third is an equiprobable bit disguised as a four-symbol source, with entropy $1$ bit, since the terms with zero probability count as zero.

Option (a) confuses a spread of values with uncertainty: the entropy is largest when no symbol is favored. Option (c) points to the least uncertain of the three sources, which never uses half of its alphabet. The three entropies are $2$, $\tfrac74$ and $1$ bits.

Exercise 3.3.

A bit is $1$ with probability $\tfrac14$. What is its entropy $h_2(\tfrac14)$, written in closed form?

(a) $2-\tfrac34\log_23$
(b) $\tfrac34$
(c) $\tfrac12+\tfrac34\log_23$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

By the definition of the binary entropy function, $h_2(\tfrac14)=-\tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14-\tfrac34\log_2\tfrac34$. The first term is $\tfrac14\cdot2=\tfrac12$, and since $\log_2\tfrac34=\log_23-2$ the second is $\tfrac34(2-\log_23)=\tfrac32-\tfrac34\log_23$. Adding, $h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$, which is about $0.811$ bits with $\log_23\approx1.585$, in agreement with Table 3.1 of the notes.

Option (c) has the sign of the logarithm wrong, and it would exceed one bit, which no binary entropy can. Option (b) is the probability of the more likely value, not an entropy. The value $0.811$ is well below one bit, although $\tfrac14$ is not that far from $\tfrac12$: the binary entropy function is flat near its maximum.

Exercise 3.4.

Which of the following statements about the binary entropy function is true?

(a) $h_2(\tfrac9{10})=h_2(\tfrac1{10})$, because a bit that is $1$ with probability $\tfrac9{10}$ is as uncertain as one that is $0$ with probability $\tfrac9{10}$.
(b) $h_2(\tfrac9{10})=1-h_2(\tfrac1{10})$, because the two probabilities add up to one.
(c) $h_2(\tfrac9{10})\gt h_2(\tfrac12)$, because $\tfrac9{10}$ is larger than $\tfrac12$.

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The binary entropy function is symmetric, $h_2(p)=h_2(1-p)$, because its definition $-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)$ does not change when $p$ and $1-p$ are exchanged. The uncertainty of a bit depends on how unbalanced its two values are, not on which of them is the likely one. Both $h_2(\tfrac9{10})$ and $h_2(\tfrac1{10})$ equal $0.469$ bits by Table 3.1 of the notes.

Option (b) would give $h_2(\tfrac9{10})=0.531$, which contradicts the symmetry. Option (c) is impossible, because $h_2(\tfrac12)=1$ is the maximum of the function. The symmetry is used constantly: on the bit-flipping channel, the conditional entropy $h_2(p)$ is the same for $p$ and for $1-p$.

Exercise 3.5.

A bit-flipping channel has flip probability $p=\tfrac14$, and its input is $1$ with probability $\tfrac13$. What is the conditional entropy $H(Y|X)$ of the output given the input?

(a) $h_2(\tfrac13)$
(b) $2-\tfrac34\log_23$
(c) $\tfrac13\bigl(2-\tfrac34\log_23\bigr)$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The conditional entropy is the average over the input of the entropy of each row of the channel law, $H(Y|X)=\sum_x\Pr(x)H(Y|X=x)$. On the bit-flipping channel both rows contain the probabilities $p$ and $1-p$, so both have entropy $h_2(p)$, and the average is $h_2(p)$ whatever the weights. With $p=\tfrac14$, Exercise 3.3 gives $H(Y|X)=h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$, about $0.811$ bits.

Option (c) weights the row entropy by the probability of one input only, forgetting the other row. Option (a) is the entropy of the input, which plays no role in $H(Y|X)$. The input distribution changes $H(Y)$ and the mutual information, but not the uncertainty that the channel adds.

Exercise 3.6.

A channel has input alphabet $\{0,1\}$ and output alphabet $\{0,1,2\}$. When the input is $0$, the output is $0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. When the input is $1$, the output is $1$ with probability $\tfrac14$ and $2$ with probability $\tfrac34$. The input is equiprobable. What is $H(Y|X)$?

(a) $\tfrac32-\tfrac38\log_23$
(b) $2-\tfrac34\log_23$
(c) $1$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The two rows of the channel law have different entropies. The row of the input $0$ is an equiprobable choice between two values, with entropy $1$ bit, and the row of the input $1$ has the probabilities $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac34$, with entropy $h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$. Averaging with the weights $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac12$ gives $H(Y|X)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\bigl(2-\tfrac34\log_23\bigr)=\tfrac32-\tfrac38\log_23$, about $0.906$ bits.

Option (b) is the entropy of the second row alone, and option (c) that of the first. When the rows differ, the conditional entropy depends on the input distribution, unlike on the bit-flipping channel of Exercise 3.5.

Exercise 3.7.

A constellation of $16$ points is used with probability $\tfrac12$ for one of the points and $\tfrac1{30}$ for each of the other $15$. What is the entropy of the transmitted symbol?

(a) $4$
(b) $\tfrac12+\log_2 15$
(c) $1+\tfrac12\log_2 15$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The favored point contributes $-\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12=\tfrac12$, and each of the other $15$ points contributes $\tfrac1{30}\log_230$, for a total of $\tfrac{15}{30}\log_230=\tfrac12\log_230$. Since $\log_230=1+\log_215$, the entropy is $H(X)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac12\log_215=1+\tfrac12\log_215$, about $2.95$ bits with $\log_215=\log_23+\log_25\approx3.907$.

Option (a) is the entropy of $16$ equiprobable points, the largest possible, and this distribution falls about one bit short of it. Option (b) forgets the factor $\tfrac12$ that multiplies the entropy of the $15$ rare points. A symbol of this constellation carries less than three bits of information on average, although it is chosen among $16$ values.

Exercise 3.8.

On which of the following channels is the conditional entropy $H(Y|X)$ equal to zero?

(a) Only on the bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=0$, because a channel that flips every bit adds uncertainty.
(b) On neither of them, because both have a nonzero parameter.
(c) On the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=1$ and on the bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=0$.

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The conditional entropy is zero exactly when the output is a function of the input almost surely, that is, when every row corresponding to an input of positive probability has a single entry equal to one. On the bit-flipping channel with $p=1$ the output is always the complement of the input, and on the bit-erasing channel with $p=0$ the output is always the input. In both cases $H(Y|X)=h_2(p)$ with $p\in\{0,1\}$, which is zero.

Option (a) confuses a deterministic transformation with noise: flipping every bit changes the output but adds no uncertainty, and the receiver that knows the channel simply flips it back. Option (b) forgets that $h_2(1)=0$. These two channels carry one bit per use without error, and their capacity is $1$.

Exercise 3.9.

A bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac14$ is used with equiprobable inputs. What is the mutual information $I(X;Y)$, in bits per channel use?

(a) $\tfrac14$
(b) $\tfrac34\log_23-1$
(c) $2-\tfrac34\log_23$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

With equiprobable inputs the output of the bit-flipping channel is equiprobable too, so $H(Y)=1$, and the conditional entropy is $H(Y|X)=h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$ by Exercise 3.3. Hence $I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)=1-2+\tfrac34\log_23=\tfrac34\log_23-1$, about $0.189$ bits per channel use.

Option (c) is the conditional entropy, the uncertainty added by the channel, not the information that gets through. Option (a) is the flip probability itself. A channel that flips one bit in four conveys less than a fifth of a bit per use, because the receiver cannot tell which bits were flipped.

Exercise 3.10.

The bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac14$ is now used with an input that is $1$ with probability $q=\tfrac14$. What is the mutual information $I(X;Y)$?

(a) $3-\tfrac38\log_23-\tfrac58\log_25$
(b) $\tfrac34\log_23-1$
(c) $1+\tfrac38\log_23-\tfrac58\log_25$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The output is $1$ when a $1$ is sent and not flipped or when a $0$ is sent and flipped, so $\Pr(Y=1)=\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac34+\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac38$. Its entropy is $h_2(\tfrac38)=-\tfrac38\log_2\tfrac38-\tfrac58\log_2\tfrac58=\tfrac38(3-\log_23)+\tfrac58(3-\log_25)=3-\tfrac38\log_23-\tfrac58\log_25$. Subtracting $H(Y|X)=h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$ gives $I(X;Y)=1+\tfrac38\log_23-\tfrac58\log_25$, about $0.143$ bits per channel use.

Option (a) is the output entropy $H(Y)$, about $0.954$ bits, without the subtraction. Option (b) is the mutual information with equiprobable inputs, Exercise 3.9, which is larger: the unbalanced input makes the output less uncertain without reducing the uncertainty due to the noise, and the information drops from $0.189$ to $0.143$ bits.

Exercise 3.11.

A bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=\tfrac14$ is used with equiprobable inputs. What is the mutual information?

(a) $\tfrac34$
(b) $h_2(\tfrac14)$
(c) $\tfrac14$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

By the worked bit-erasing problem of the notes, the mutual information of the bit-erasing channel is $I(X;Y)=(1-p)\,h_2(q)$ when the input is $1$ with probability $q$. With $q=\tfrac12$ and $p=\tfrac14$, this is $\tfrac34\cdot1=\tfrac34$ bits per channel use. Three bits out of four arrive intact and identify the input, and the fourth is known to be missing.

Option (c) is the erased fraction, the information that is lost rather than the information that gets through. Option (b), $h_2(\tfrac14)=0.811$, is the entropy of the erasure pattern, which is the uncertainty the channel adds, $H(Y|X)$. Compare with the bit-flipping channel of Exercise 3.9, with the same parameter: $0.75$ bits against $0.189$, because an erasure, unlike a flip, is visible.

Exercise 3.12.

A bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=\tfrac12$ is used with an input that is $1$ with probability $\tfrac14$. What is the mutual information?

(a) $2-\tfrac34\log_23$
(b) $\tfrac12$
(c) $1-\tfrac38\log_23$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

By the worked bit-erasing problem of the notes, $I(X;Y)=(1-p)\,h_2(q)$ with $q=\tfrac14$ and $p=\tfrac12$. By Exercise 3.3, $h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$, so $I(X;Y)=\tfrac12\bigl(2-\tfrac34\log_23\bigr)=1-\tfrac38\log_23$, about $0.406$ bits per channel use.

Option (b) is the mutual information with equiprobable inputs, the capacity $1-p$ of this channel, which the unbalanced input does not reach. Option (a) is $h_2(\tfrac14)$ itself, the information the input carries before half of it is erased. The erasure channel transmits the fraction $1-p$ of whatever entropy the input has.

Exercise 3.13.

A bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=\tfrac14$ is used with equiprobable inputs. What is the conditional entropy $H(X|Y)$ of the input given the output?

(a) $h_2(\tfrac14)$
(b) $\tfrac14$
(c) $\tfrac34$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The mutual information is symmetric in its two variables, $I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)=H(X)-H(X|Y)$, so $H(X|Y)=H(X)-I(X;Y)$. By Exercise 3.11, $I(X;Y)=\tfrac34$, and $H(X)=1$ for an equiprobable bit, so $H(X|Y)=\tfrac14$ bits. This is the uncertainty about the input that remains after observing the output: nothing when the bit arrives, one full bit when it is erased, and the erasure happens with probability $\tfrac14$.

Option (c) is the mutual information, the uncertainty that the output removes rather than the one it leaves. Option (a) is $H(Y|X)$, the entropy of the erasure pattern, which is a different conditional entropy. On the bit-flipping channel with equiprobable inputs, the two conditional entropies coincide because $H(X)=H(Y)=1$.

Exercise 3.14.

A memoryless channel has input $X\in\{0,1\}$ and output $Y\in\{0,1,?\}$, where $?$ is an erasure. Each input is received correctly with probability $\tfrac58$, flipped with probability $\tfrac18$ and erased with probability $\tfrac14$. What is the conditional entropy $H(Y|X)$, for any input distribution?

(a) $\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac34\log_23$
(b) $\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac58\log_25$
(c) $\tfrac32$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

Both rows of the channel law contain the probabilities $\tfrac58$, $\tfrac18$ and $\tfrac14$, so both have the same entropy, and $H(Y|X)$ equals that entropy whatever the input distribution. It is $-\tfrac58\log_2\tfrac58-\tfrac18\log_2\tfrac18-\tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14=\tfrac58(3-\log_25)+\tfrac38+\tfrac12=\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac58\log_25$, about $1.30$ bits.

Option (c), $\tfrac32$, is the row entropy of the channel of Seminar 4, whose rows are $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac14$. Option (a) is the entropy of the output when the input is equiprobable, computed in Exercise 3.15. The row entropy here is lower than in Seminar 4 because flips are rarer, and the channel is correspondingly better.

Exercise 3.15.

The channel of Exercise 3.14 is used with equiprobable inputs. What is the mutual information $I(X;Y)$?

(a) $\tfrac58\log_25-\tfrac34\log_23$
(b) $\tfrac34$
(c) $\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac58\log_25$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

By the output-law formula, $\Pr(Y=0)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac58+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac38$, and by symmetry $\Pr(Y=1)=\tfrac38$, while $\Pr(Y=?)=\tfrac14$. The output entropy is $-2\cdot\tfrac38\log_2\tfrac38-\tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14=\tfrac34(3-\log_23)+\tfrac12=\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac34\log_23$, and subtracting the conditional entropy $\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac58\log_25$ of Exercise 3.14 gives $I(X;Y)=\tfrac58\log_25-\tfrac34\log_23$, about $0.262$ bits per channel use.

Option (b) is the mutual information of the bit-erasing channel with the same erasure probability $\tfrac14$ and no flips, which this channel does not reach: a received bit may have been flipped, so even an unerased output leaves some uncertainty. Option (c) is $H(Y|X)$. The channel of Seminar 4, with flips twice as likely, conveys only $\tfrac54-\tfrac34\log_23\approx0.061$ bits per use.

Exercise 3.16.

A channel has input alphabet $\{0,1\}$ and output alphabet $\{0,1,2\}$. When the input is $0$, the output is $0$ with probability $\tfrac34$ and $1$ with probability $\tfrac14$. When the input is $1$, the output is $1$ with probability $\tfrac14$ and $2$ with probability $\tfrac34$. The input is equiprobable. What is the mutual information?

(a) $\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac34\log_23$
(b) $\tfrac14$
(c) $\tfrac34$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

By the output-law formula, $\Pr(Y=0)=\Pr(Y=2)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34=\tfrac38$ and $\Pr(Y=1)=\tfrac14$, so $H(Y)=\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac34\log_23$ as in Exercise 3.15. Both rows have the probabilities $\tfrac34$ and $\tfrac14$, so $H(Y|X)=h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$, and $I(X;Y)=\tfrac{11}4-2=\tfrac34$ bits per channel use.

The result has a simple reading. The outputs $0$ and $2$ reveal the input with certainty, and the output $1$, which has probability $\tfrac14$, says nothing about it. The channel is a bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $\tfrac14$ under another name, with the mutual information $1-p=\tfrac34$ of Exercise 3.11. Option (a) is $H(Y)$ and option (b) is the probability of the uninformative output.

Exercise 3.17.

A channel adds to its input a noise $Z$, independent of the input, that is uniform on $[0,1)$, with density $f_Z(z)=1$ for $0\leq z\lt 1$ and $0$ otherwise, and the input is $X\in\{0,\tfrac12\}$, equiprobable. What are the values of the output density $f_Y(y)$ on the intervals $[0,\tfrac12)$, $[\tfrac12,1)$ and $[1,\tfrac32)$, in this order?

(a) $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac14$
(b) $\tfrac12$, $1$ and $\tfrac12$
(c) $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac12$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The channel law is the density $W(y|x)=f_Z(y-x)$, equal to $1$ for $x\leq y\lt x+1$, and the output density is the sum rule with a density in the place of $\Pr(y)$, $f_Y(y)=\tfrac12W(y|0)+\tfrac12W(y|\tfrac12)$. On $[0,\tfrac12)$ only the input $0$ contributes, on $[\tfrac12,1)$ both inputs do, and on $[1,\tfrac32)$ only the input $\tfrac12$. Hence $f_Y$ equals $\tfrac12$, $1$ and $\tfrac12$ on the three intervals, and the areas $\tfrac14+\tfrac12+\tfrac14$ add to $1$, as a density must. No density of $X$ is needed: the input is discrete and the sum over $x$ stays a sum.

Option (a) halves every value and has total area $\tfrac12$. Option (c) forgets that both inputs contribute on the middle interval and has area $\tfrac34$. The output is a mixture of two shifted copies of the noise density, and where the copies overlap the output is more likely.

Exercise 3.18.

A bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac14$ is used with equiprobable inputs. What is the conditional entropy $H(X|Y)$ of the input given the output?

(a) $2-\tfrac34\log_23$
(b) $\tfrac14$
(c) $\tfrac34\log_23-1$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

By the symmetry of the mutual information, $H(X|Y)=H(X)-I(X;Y)$. With $H(X)=1$ and $I(X;Y)=\tfrac34\log_23-1$ from Exercise 3.9, $H(X|Y)=2-\tfrac34\log_23=h_2(\tfrac14)$, about $0.811$ bits. On this channel the two conditional entropies coincide: given the output, the input is the output with probability $\tfrac34$ and its complement with probability $\tfrac14$, the same odds as the output given the input.

Option (c) is the mutual information. Option (b) is the flip probability, which is the parameter of the uncertainty and not the uncertainty itself. Compare with the erasure channel of Exercise 3.13, where $H(X|Y)=\tfrac14$: at the same parameter, a received flip leaves far more uncertainty than an erasure, because it is not visible.

Exercise 3.19.

For the channel and the input of Exercise 3.17, with noise uniform on $[0,1)$ and $X\in\{0,\tfrac12\}$ equiprobable, what is the mutual information $I(X;Y)$?

(a) $1$
(b) $\tfrac34$
(c) $\tfrac12$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

For additive noise, $I(X;Y)=h(Y)-h(Z)$, and a uniform noise on an interval of length $1$ has $h(Z)=\log_21=0$. The output density of Exercise 3.17 is piecewise constant, and its differential entropy is a sum of three terms, length times value times minus the logarithm of the value: $h(Y)=-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot\log_21-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12=\tfrac14+0+\tfrac14=\tfrac12$. Hence $I(X;Y)=\tfrac12$ bits per channel use.

The value has a simple reading. An output in $[\tfrac12,1)$, which happens with probability $\tfrac12$, is equally likely under both inputs and says nothing, as an erasure does, and any other output identifies the input. The channel is a bit-erasing channel with $p=\tfrac12$, whose mutual information with equiprobable inputs is $1-p=\tfrac12$. Option (a) would need the two supports not to overlap, that is, a spacing of at least $1$. Option (b) is the value for a spacing of $\tfrac34$, where the erasure probability is $\tfrac14$.

Exercise 3.20.

The noise is uniform on $[0,1)$, as in Exercise 3.17, and the input now takes the three values $0$, $1$ and $2$ with equal probabilities. What is the mutual information?

(a) $1$
(b) $\log_23$
(c) $2$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The three channel laws $W(y|0)$, $W(y|1)$ and $W(y|2)$ equal $1$ on $[0,1)$, $[1,2)$ and $[2,3)$, which do not overlap, so the output density is $\tfrac13$ on the whole of $[0,3)$ and $h(Y)=\log_23$. With $h(Z)=\log_21=0$, $I(X;Y)=\log_23$, about $1.585$ bits per channel use, which is $H(X)$: the output determines the input, since each interval of length $1$ belongs to one input only.

This channel is noisy and yet loses nothing, because the noise is bounded and the points are spaced by its width. It is the continuous counterpart of the noiseless channel, and the bound $I(X;Y)\leq H(X)$ of Proposition 3.4 of the notes is reached. Option (a) is the value for two points, and option (c) would need four points spaced by $1$. With points spaced by less than $1$ the supports overlap and the mutual information drops below $\log_2M$, as in Exercise 3.19.

Exercise 3.21.

A bit-flipping channel and a bit-erasing channel both have parameter $p=\tfrac1{10}$. Which has the larger capacity?

(a) The bit-erasing channel, with $C=\tfrac9{10}$ against $C=1-h_2(\tfrac1{10})\approx0.531$.
(b) The bit-flipping channel, because a flipped bit still carries a value.
(c) Both have capacity $\tfrac9{10}$, because both damage one bit in ten.

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The capacities are $C=1-h_2(p)$ for the bit-flipping channel and $C=1-p$ for the bit-erasing channel, both achieved by equiprobable inputs. With $p=\tfrac1{10}$ and $h_2(\tfrac1{10})=0.469$ from Table 3.1 of the notes, the capacities are $0.531$ and $0.9$ bits per channel use. The erasure channel is better, because the receiver knows which bit is missing, whereas a flipped bit looks like a correct one.

Option (b) has the comparison backwards: the value of a flipped bit is misleading, not informative. Option (c) ignores that $h_2(p)\gt p$ for $0\lt p\lt \tfrac12$, so a flip costs more than an erasure. For $0\lt p\leq\tfrac12$, the bit-erasing channel has strictly larger capacity.

Exercise 3.22.

What is the capacity of the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac34$?

(a) $\tfrac14$
(b) $\tfrac34\log_23-1$
(c) $0$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The capacity of the bit-flipping channel is $C=1-h_2(p)$, and by the symmetry of the binary entropy function $h_2(\tfrac34)=h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$. Hence $C=\tfrac34\log_23-1$, about $0.189$ bits per channel use, the same as for $p=\tfrac14$ in Exercise 3.9. A receiver that knows the channel flips every received bit and faces a channel with flip probability $\tfrac14$.

Option (c) would be the capacity at $p=\tfrac12$, the only value of $p$ at which the channel is useless. Option (a) is the probability that a bit arrives unchanged, not a capacity. The capacity of the bit-flipping channel decreases from $1$ at $p=0$ to $0$ at $p=\tfrac12$ and rises again to $1$ at $p=1$.

Exercise 3.23.

A code of rate $R=\tfrac14$ is proposed for the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac14$. Can its error probability be made as small as desired by taking long enough codes of that rate?

(a) Yes, because the rate $\tfrac14$ is below $1-p=\tfrac34$, the fraction of bits that arrive unchanged.
(b) Yes, because the rate equals the flip probability, so the redundancy compensates the flips.
(c) No, because the rate $\tfrac14$ is above the capacity $\tfrac34\log_23-1\approx0.189$.

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

By the channel coding theorem, reliable transmission is possible for $R\lt C$ and impossible for $R\gt C$. The capacity of this channel is $C=1-h_2(\tfrac14)=\tfrac34\log_23-1\approx0.189$ bits per channel use, by Exercise 3.9. Since $\tfrac14=0.25\gt 0.189$, no sequence of codes of rate $\tfrac14$ can drive the error probability to zero.

Option (a) uses the wrong threshold: $1-p$ is the capacity of the erasure channel, not of the flipping channel, and a flipped bit costs more than an erased one. Option (b) has no basis, since the rate and the flip probability are quantities of different nature. The largest reliable rates on this channel are just below $0.189$, less than one bit in five.

Exercise 3.24.

A code of rate $R=\tfrac35$ is proposed for the bit-erasing channel with erasure probability $p=\tfrac13$. Can its error probability be made as small as desired by taking long enough codes of that rate?

(a) Yes, because $\tfrac35$ is below the capacity $1-p=\tfrac23$.
(b) No, because $\tfrac35$ is above the erasure probability $\tfrac13$.
(c) No, because with a third of the bits erased only rates below $\tfrac13$ are reliable.

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The capacity of the bit-erasing channel is $C=1-p=\tfrac23\approx0.667$ bits per channel use, and the proposed rate $\tfrac35=0.6$ is below it. By the channel coding theorem there are codes of rate $\tfrac35$ and large enough length with error probability as small as desired. The theorem promises their existence, not that any particular code of that rate is good.

Option (b) compares the rate with the wrong quantity: the erasure probability is not a limit on the rate. Option (c) confuses the lost fraction with the surviving one. Codes of any rate strictly below $\tfrac23$ can be made reliable here, because two bits in three arrive intact and the code can be designed so that the surviving bits determine the message.

Exercise 3.25.

On the bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac1{10}$, whose capacity is $1-h_2(\tfrac1{10})\approx0.531$, the repetition code of length $3$ has rate $\tfrac13$ and error probability $\tfrac7{250}$. What does the channel coding theorem say about this situation?

(a) Every code of rate $\tfrac13$ on this channel has error probability at most $\tfrac7{250}$.
(b) There are codes of rate at least $\tfrac13$, of larger length, with error probability as small as desired.
(c) The rate $\tfrac13$ cannot be made reliable, because the repetition code of that rate has a positive error probability.

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The theorem concerns the best codes, not a given one. Since $\tfrac13\lt 0.531$, for every target error probability there is a code of rate at least $\tfrac13$ and sufficiently large length that meets it. The repetition code is one code of that rate, and a poor one: it reduces its error only by reducing its rate, and Table 3.3 of the notes shows that it needs $n=23$ and rate $\tfrac1{23}$ to reach $10^{-6}$.

Option (a) reads the theorem as a guarantee for every code, which it is not: a code of rate below the capacity may be as bad as one likes. Option (c) draws a conclusion about the rate from a single code, whereas the theorem says that the limitation is in that code, not in the rate. The good codes of rate $\tfrac13$ are long and are not repetition codes.

Exercise 3.26.

Three quantities measured in bits per channel use appear in Topic 3: the rate $R$ of a code, the mutual information $I(X;Y)$ and the capacity $C$. For a channel with finite alphabets and no constraint on the input, which of them depends on the channel alone?

(a) The mutual information $I(X;Y)$, because it is computed from the channel law.
(b) The rate $R=k/n$, because it is fixed before the channel is used.
(c) The capacity $C$, because it is the largest mutual information over all the input distributions.

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The capacity is defined as the maximum of $I(X;Y)$ over the input distributions, so the input has been optimized away and $C$ is a property of the channel alone. It is the threshold of the rates at which reliable transmission is possible with the best possible input.

The mutual information of option (a) depends on the channel and on the input distribution: on the bit-flipping channel with $p=\tfrac14$ it is $0.189$ bits with equiprobable inputs and $0.143$ bits with an input that is $1$ with probability $\tfrac14$, by Exercises 3.9 and 3.10. The rate of option (b) belongs to the code, not to the channel: it is what the transmitter attempts to send, and the theorem compares it with $C$.

Exercise 3.27.

A bit-erasing channel has erasure probability $p=\tfrac12$. What is the smallest number of channel uses per information bit that a reliable code can approach on this channel, for long codes?

(a) $4$
(b) $2$
(c) $1$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The capacity is $C=1-p=\tfrac12$ bits per channel use, and reliable codes have rates $R=k/n\lt C$. The number of channel uses per information bit is $n/k=1/R$, which is therefore larger than $1/C=2$, and the coding theorem says that rates arbitrarily close to $C$ are reliable, so the ratio can approach $2$ from above.

Option (c) would be a rate of one bit per use, impossible when half of the uses are erased. Option (a) corresponds to a rate $\tfrac14$, which is reliable but wasteful, since it uses twice as many channel uses as necessary. Intuitively, every information bit needs two uses because only one of them survives on average, and good codes make the surviving uses count.

Exercise 3.28.

A binary channel never flips a $1$, and flips a $0$ into a $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$. Its input is equiprobable. What is the mutual information?

(a) $\tfrac12$
(b) $\log_2\tfrac54$
(c) $\tfrac32-\tfrac34\log_23$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The output is $1$ when a $1$ is sent or when a $0$ is sent and flipped, so $\Pr(Y=1)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac34$ and $H(Y)=h_2(\tfrac14)=2-\tfrac34\log_23$. Given $X=1$ the output is certain, and given $X=0$ it is an equiprobable bit, so $H(Y|X)=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac12$. Hence $I(X;Y)=\tfrac32-\tfrac34\log_23$, about $0.311$ bits per channel use.

Option (b), $\log_2\tfrac54\approx0.322$, is the capacity of this channel, reached by Example 3.8 of the notes with an input that is $0$ with probability $\tfrac25$, slightly less often than the other value because $0$ is the input that suffers the noise. Option (a) is $H(Y|X)$. The equiprobable input falls only $0.011$ bits short of the capacity.

Exercise 3.29.

For which values of the flip probability $p$ is the capacity of the bit-flipping channel equal to zero?

(a) Only for $p=\tfrac12$.
(b) For $p=0$ and for $p=1$.
(c) For $p=1$ only, because then every bit is wrong.

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The capacity is $C=1-h_2(p)$, and the binary entropy function equals $1$ only at $p=\tfrac12$, where the output is independent of the input and no input distribution conveys anything. For every other value of $p$, the receiver can extract some information: the closer $p$ is to $0$ or to $1$, the more.

Option (b) names the two values at which the capacity is largest, $C=1$, since $h_2(0)=h_2(1)=0$. Option (c) overlooks that a channel which flips every bit is a noiseless channel followed by an inversion, which the receiver undoes: a bit that is always wrong is as good as a bit that is always right, once one knows it.

Exercise 3.30.

A bit-flipping channel with flip probability $p=\tfrac1{10}$ has capacity $1-h_2(\tfrac1{10})\approx0.531$ bits per channel use. At rates approaching the capacity, how many information bits per $1000$ channel uses can a reliable code carry at most, approximately?

(a) About $1000$, if the code is long enough to correct the flips.
(b) Fewer than $540$, about $530$.
(c) About $900$, because on average only $100$ bits are flipped.

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

As the blocklength grows and the rate approaches capacity from below, the number of information bits per $1000$ channel uses approaches $1000C\approx531$. This is an asymptotic normalization, not a guarantee for a block of length $1000$. Fewer than $540$ bits, about $530$, is the right order.

Option (c) counts the bits that arrive correct, but the receiver does not know which ones they are, and the redundancy needed to tell them apart costs far more than the $100$ flipped bits: the price is $h_2(p)$ per use, about $469$ bits, not $p$. Option (a) ignores the capacity altogether. Long codes do correct the flips, but only at rates below $0.531$.

Exercise 3.31.

A random variable is uniform on the interval $[0,8]$. What is its differential entropy, in bits?

(a) $8$
(b) $1$
(c) $3$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

A uniform density on an interval of length $a$ equals $\tfrac1a$ there, so the differential entropy is $-\int_0^a\tfrac1a\log_2\tfrac1a\,\mathrm du=\log_2a$. With $a=8$, $h(X)=\log_28=3$ bits.

Option (a) is the length of the interval, not its logarithm. Option (b) would be the differential entropy of a uniform variable on an interval of length $2$. The differential entropy of a uniform variable grows by one bit every time the interval doubles, and it is zero for an interval of length $1$.

Exercise 3.32.

A random variable is uniform on the interval $[0,\tfrac14]$. What is its differential entropy, in bits?

(a) $-2$
(b) $2$
(c) $0$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

By the formula of Exercise 3.31, the differential entropy of a uniform variable on an interval of length $a$ is $\log_2a$, and with $a=\tfrac14$ this is $\log_2\tfrac14=-2$ bits. The density equals $4$ on the interval, larger than $1$, and the logarithm of a density larger than $1$ is positive, which makes the differential entropy negative.

Option (b) has the sign wrong, and it would be the differential entropy of a uniform variable on an interval of length $4$. Option (c) corresponds to length $1$. Unlike the entropy of a discrete variable, the differential entropy can be negative, and its sign depends on the units in which the variable is measured.

Exercise 3.33.

Two real Gaussian random variables have variances $4$ and $1$. By how many bits does the differential entropy of the first exceed that of the second?

(a) $4$
(b) $2$
(c) $1$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The differential entropy of a real Gaussian variable of variance $\sigma^2$ is $h=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)$. The difference between the two is $\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\cdot4)-\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\cdot1)=\tfrac12\log_24=1$ bit. The factor $2\pi e$ cancels in every difference.

Option (b) would be the difference for a circular complex Gaussian, whose differential entropy is $\log_2(\pi e\sigma^2)$. Its dependence on the variance has no factor $\tfrac12$, and the additive constant cancels in the difference. Option (a) is the ratio of the variances, not the difference of logarithms. Multiplying the variance by $4$ doubles the standard deviation, and a Gaussian twice as wide has one more bit of differential entropy, just as a uniform variable gains one bit when its interval doubles in length.

Exercise 3.34.

A real random variable $X$ has differential entropy $h(X)$. What is the differential entropy of $Y=4X$?

(a) $h(X)+2$
(b) $4\,h(X)$
(c) $h(X)+4$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

Scaling a variable by $a$ stretches its density by a factor $|a|$ in width and divides it by $|a|$ in height, and the differential entropy changes by $\log_2|a|$. With $a=4$, $h(4X)=h(X)+\log_24=h(X)+2$ bits. For a uniform variable on $[0,1]$, for instance, $h(X)=0$ and $4X$ is uniform on $[0,4]$, with $h(4X)=2$.

Option (b) treats the differential entropy as if it were proportional to the variable, and option (c) adds the factor instead of its logarithm. The rule shows why the differential entropy depends on the units: measuring the same quantity in units four times smaller multiplies every value by $4$ and adds two bits.

Exercise 3.35.

A channel adds to its input a noise $Z$, independent of the input and uniform on $[0,2)$, with density $\tfrac12$ there and $0$ elsewhere, and the input is $X\in\{0,1\}$, equiprobable. What is the mutual information?

(a) $\tfrac32$
(b) $\tfrac12$
(c) $1$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The channel laws $W(y|0)$ and $W(y|1)$ equal $\tfrac12$ on $[0,2)$ and on $[1,3)$, so the output density $f_Y(y)=\tfrac12W(y|0)+\tfrac12W(y|1)$ equals $\tfrac14$ on $[0,1)$, $\tfrac12$ on $[1,2)$ and $\tfrac14$ on $[2,3)$, with total area $1$. Its differential entropy is $h(Y)=2\cdot1\cdot\tfrac14\cdot2+1\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac32$, and the noise, uniform on an interval of length $2$, has $h(Z)=\log_22=1$. Hence $I(X;Y)=h(Y)-h(Z)=\tfrac12$ bits per channel use.

Option (a) is $h(Y)$ alone, which forgets to subtract the entropy of the noise, nonzero here. Option (c) would need the spacing of the inputs to equal the width of the noise. The channel is that of Exercise 3.19 with every quantity scaled by $2$: by the scaling rule of Exercise 3.34, $h(Y)$ and $h(Z)$ both gain one bit and their difference is the same $\tfrac12$. Mutual information does not depend on the units, although differential entropies do.

Exercise 3.36.

An exponential random variable has mean $\mu=2$. What is its differential entropy, in bits?

(a) $1+\log_2e$
(b) $\log_2e$
(c) $2$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The differential entropy of an exponential variable of mean $\mu$ is $h=\log_2(e\mu)$, computed in Problem 3.12 of the notes from the logarithm of the density, $-\log_2\mu-\tfrac x\mu\log_2e$, whose expectation is $-\log_2\mu-\log_2e$. Option (b) is the differential entropy of an exponential variable of mean $1$. Option (c) is the mean itself. Doubling the mean of an exponential variable scales it by $2$ and adds one bit, in agreement with the scaling rule of Exercise 3.34.

Exercise 3.37.

Three real random variables have variance $1$: a Gaussian, a uniform variable on $[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]$ and a third variable with some other density, not Gaussian. Which of them has the largest differential entropy?

(a) The uniform variable, because its density is flat.
(b) The Gaussian variable, whatever the third density is.
(c) It depends on the third density, which may exceed both.

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

Among the real random variables of a given variance, the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy, $\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)$, by the maximum-entropy theorem of the notes. With $\sigma^2=1$ this is $\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e)\approx2.05$ bits. The uniform variable on an interval of length $2\sqrt3$, which indeed has variance $\tfrac{(2\sqrt3)^2}{12}=1$, has $h=\log_2(2\sqrt3)=1+\tfrac12\log_23\approx1.79$ bits, below the Gaussian.

Option (a) confuses a flat density with a maximal entropy, which holds for a given interval, not for a given variance. Option (c) contradicts the theorem, which bounds every density of variance $1$ by the Gaussian value. This is the fact behind the Gaussian capacity: to make the output as uncertain as the energy allows, the input must be Gaussian.

Exercise 3.38.

For which variance $\sigma^2$ is the differential entropy of a real Gaussian random variable equal to zero?

(a) $\sigma^2=\tfrac1{2\pi}$
(b) $\sigma^2=1$
(c) $\sigma^2=\tfrac1{2\pi e}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The differential entropy $\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)$ vanishes when the argument of the logarithm is $1$, that is, for $\sigma^2=\tfrac1{2\pi e}\approx0.059$. For smaller variances the differential entropy is negative, and for larger ones it is positive. A Gaussian of variance $1$ has $h\approx2.05$ bits.

Option (b) is the natural guess from the discrete case, where a single value has zero entropy, but the differential entropy has no such anchor. Option (a) forgets the factor $e$ that comes from the expectation of the quadratic term. Exercise 3.39.

A real Gaussian channel has the average-energy constraint $\mathrm E(X^2)\leq E_{\rm s}=7$ and noise variance $\sigma^2=1$. What is its capacity, in bits per channel use?

(a) $3$
(b) $\tfrac32$
(c) $\tfrac72$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The capacity of the real Gaussian channel under the energy constraint $\mathrm E(X^2)\leq E_{\rm s}$ is $C=\tfrac12\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ with $\mathrm{SNR}=E_{\rm s}/\sigma^2=7$. Hence $C=\tfrac12\log_28=\tfrac32$ bits per channel use, achieved by a zero-mean Gaussian input of variance $7$.

Option (a) omits the factor $\tfrac12$ of the real channel, and it would be the capacity of a complex channel with the same SNR. Option (c) is half the SNR, a quantity with no meaning here. Each doubling of $1+\mathrm{SNR}$ adds half a bit on the real channel, and $1+7=8$ is three doublings.

Exercise 3.40.

A complex Gaussian channel has the average-energy constraint $\mathrm E(|X|^2)\leq E_{\rm s}=9$ and total noise variance $\sigma^2=3$. What is its capacity, in bits per channel use?

(a) $1$
(b) $4$
(c) $2$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The capacity of the complex Gaussian channel is $C=\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ with $\mathrm{SNR}=E_{\rm s}/\sigma^2=3$, so $C=\log_24=2$ bits per channel use. The two real components of a complex use, each with energy $\tfrac92$ and noise variance $\tfrac32$, have the same SNR as the whole and carry one bit each, by the real formula $\tfrac12\log_24=1$.

Exercise 3.42.

Exercise 3.45.

Exercise 3.48.

On a real Gaussian channel at $20$ dB, Table 3.5 of the notes gives $1.00$ bits per channel use for equiprobable 2-PAM, a rounded value, and a capacity of $3.33$. Can uncoded 2-PAM, at a rate of exactly $1$ bit per channel use, be made reliable at this SNR?

(a) Yes, because the capacity $3.33$ is far above $1$.
(b) No, because the mutual information of 2-PAM is strictly below $1$ at every finite SNR.
(c) Yes, because the table gives $1.00$ and the rate is $1$.

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The table value $1.00$ is rounded. A rate of exactly $1$ is not below the mutual information, and the coding theorem gives no reliable codes at that rate with this input. In fact, uncoded 2-PAM has the error probability $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}/2})$ of Topic 1, which is small at $20$ dB but not zero, and no code at rate $1$ can improve on it, since there is no redundancy to use.

Option (a) uses the capacity, which concerns the best input, not 2-PAM. Option (c) takes a rounded table entry as an exact inequality. Saturation is the reason why a constellation with $\log_2M$ larger than the target rate is needed.

Exercise 3.49.

On a complex Gaussian channel at $0$ dB, Table 3.5 of the notes gives a capacity of $1.00$ bits per channel use and a mutual information of $0.97$ for equiprobable QPSK. A binary code of rate $r=\tfrac12$ is followed by QPSK. Can the transmission be made reliable?

(a) Yes, because the binary rate $\tfrac12$ is below $0.97$.
(b) Yes, because the rate $R=1$ equals the capacity $1.00$.
(c) No, because the rate $R=r\log_24=1$ is above the $0.97$ of QPSK.

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

Each QPSK symbol carries $\log_24=2$ coded bits, so $R=r\log_2M=\tfrac12\cdot2=1$ information bit per channel use, and the threshold for the equiprobable QPSK input is its mutual information, $0.97$. Since $1\gt 0.97$, no sequence of codes of this kind can make the error probability as small as desired at this SNR.

Option (a) compares the binary rate $r$, which counts information bits per coded bit, with a quantity in bits per channel use, and the two are not comparable. Option (b) looks at the capacity, which assumes a Gaussian input, and the coding theorem says nothing at a rate equal to the capacity anyway. At $5$ dB the same scheme becomes feasible, because QPSK then carries $1.72$ bits per use.

Exercise 3.50.

An additive exponential noise channel has mean noise $\mu$, and its inputs are energies with mean at most $E_{\rm s}=3\mu$. What is the largest mutual information that an input satisfying the constraint can reach?

(a) $\log_23$ bits per channel use.
(b) $2$ bits per channel use.
(c) $1$ bit per channel use.

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

Problem 3.17 of the notes shows that $I(X;Y)\leq\log_2(1+E_{\rm s}/\mu)$ for every input with $\mathrm E(X)\leq E_{\rm s}$, because the output is nonnegative with mean at most $E_{\rm s}+\mu$ and the exponential has the largest differential entropy for a given mean. With $E_{\rm s}=3\mu$ the bound is $\log_24=2$ bits per channel use, and the remark after the problem says that it is reached, by an input that is zero with probability $\tfrac14$ and otherwise exponential with mean $4\mu$.

Option (a) forgets the $1$ inside the logarithm, and option (c) applies the factor $\tfrac12$ of the real Gaussian channel, which has no place here. The formula looks like the complex Gaussian one, but the SNR is a ratio of energies to the first power and the best input is neither Gaussian nor exponential.
